Part 1

The requirement, as given

This is the brief exactly as the FDE receives it. Everything after it works backwards from these words to the bot that The Gen Academy built (repository 2A-Enterprise-QandA-Bot).

Project 1: Enterprise Policy Q&A Bot

Description

Build a RAG-powered Q&A system over real enterprise documents — HR policies, compliance manuals, product documentation, or onboarding guides. Use a no-code/low-code tool or a guided RAG starter (e.g., Pinecone + LangChain template) to ingest your documents, chunk and embed them, and stand up a question-answering interface. Then stress-test it with 15 questions including edge cases: ambiguous queries, questions that span multiple documents, and questions the knowledge base simply can’t answer. Document where retrieval succeeds, where it fails, and why.

Best For

All profiles — PMs use product docs, finance analysts use regulatory filings, consultants use client knowledge bases, engineers use technical documentation. Everyone has documents they wish they could query.

Part 2

FDE analysis: from the brief to the design

Imagine ACME Corporation hands you this brief on day one. You are the forward deployed engineer: you sit with their teams, find out what they actually need, and ship something that works in their environment. This part walks the route from the brief to 2A's design: the habits, the people, the questions, and how each answer becomes a design decision.

How to read this part. The code and the solution doc show what was built, and the doc's “Key Design Decisions” table gives some of the reasons. No interviews were recorded (this was a course brief, not a customer engagement), so the people, questions and answers below are a reconstruction: what an FDE would have needed to ask to arrive at this design. Every statement about 2A carries a tag:

Documented stated in the solution doc or README In code visible in the code; the reason is not written down Inferred our reading of what the build implies; neither the doc nor the code says it directly ★ ask in your first meeting with that team

2A in one paragraph. The three manuals are cut into chunks of about 512 characters, turned into vectors, and stored in Pinecone, one namespace (a separate partition of the index) per document family. For each question, a router (one LLM call) picks a namespace; the retriever fetches the top-k chunks, the k most similar to the question (4, or 2 from each namespace for general); a grader (one LLM call per chunk) drops chunks it judges irrelevant; the generator writes the answer; and a checker asks whether every claim is in the chunks. A check that cannot run can fail open (let the answer through) or fail closed (refuse). A re-ranker is a small model that re-scores retrieved chunks against the question. The golden set is the agreed list of test questions with expected answers and sources. 2A's edge cases are Q11 (remote work and data security), Q12 (today's stock price), Q13 (“Tell me about policies.”), Q14 (API uptime and incident response) and Q15 (the IRS 401k limit, which is not in the documents).

2.1How an FDE reads a brief

  1. Find the real deliverable. The last sentence of the description, “Document where retrieval succeeds, where it fails, and why”, says the output is an analysis; the bot is the instrument that produces it. So the evaluation is designed first, not last.
  2. Ask what a wrong answer costs before choosing any tool. A wrong API rate limit costs a developer ten minutes. A wrong data-breach deadline is a regulatory failure. That cost decides how much checking to build. 2A's relevance grader, answer checker and fixed refusal sentence are that kind of checking, though 2A never states the cost.
  3. Turn words into examples. “Ambiguous”, “span multiple documents” and “can’t answer” mean nothing until they are real questions with expected answers. The 15 questions are the specification, not a test bolted on at the end.
  4. Separate what is said, implied and unknown. “Stand up a question-answering interface” is said. “Show sources so people can check” is implied. “Who may read the compliance manual” is unknown, and the unknowns are where projects fail.
  5. Write assumptions down and get them confirmed. Building on an assumption is normal. Building on one nobody knows you made is how a demo becomes an incident. Section 2.6 lists the ones 2A made silently.
  6. Start simple; add a part only when a measured failure or a stated risk asks for it. The solution doc compares naive RAG, a LangGraph pipeline and full agentic RAG. An FDE builds the naive one first, runs the 15 questions, and lets the failures justify quality parts such as the router. Safety parts that a stated risk demands (the refusal rule and the checker because wrong answers are costly, a login because some documents are restricted) are built from the start: 15 questions cannot show a failure that happens once in 500 answers.

2.7The stress test, designed as an FDE would

The description's last sentence asks where retrieval succeeds, where it fails, and why. In the engagement this is designed before any build (step 4 in 2.8); it sits here because its failure causes refer to the parts chosen in 2.5. Start by checking what 2A's evaluate.py can actually catch.

What the 15-question run can and cannot fail on

  • 15 routing checks: the 4 questions that expect general (Q11–Q14) pass whatever the route, so 11 can fail.
  • 15 answerability checks: the 13 answerable questions pass whatever the answer says, even the refusal sentence, so a bot that refused everything would score 15/15. Only 2 can fail (Q12 and Q15), and they are checked by phrase matching. One accepted phrase is “knowledge base”, so an answer that begins “According to the knowledge base, the IRS limit is…” would pass.
  • So 17 of the 30 checks pass whatever the bot answers (only a crash stops them), and retrieval itself, whether the right chunk came back, is never checked.
  • The routing check reads the final namespace, which a retry overwrites with general, and the chunks retrieved before grading are thrown away. So the run cannot tell a routing miss from a retrieval miss or from over-filtering.
  • No results are published either: evaluate.py writes evaluation_report.json locally, but the file is listed in .gitignore, and the solution doc gives the expected answers, not a run's results. The brief's “where it fails, and why” has no artefact in the repository.

An FDE's version keeps the 15 questions and changes what is recorded and scored:

  1. Each question carries a category, the expected behaviour (answer, refuse or ask back), the expected facts and the expected source section, signed off by the document owner.
  2. Each run records every stage (the router's first label, the chunks before and after grading, each checker verdict) and scores a retrieval hit (did the expected section reach the generator?), answer correctness (are the facts there, with nothing contradicting them?), groundedness, a correct refusal, latency and the number of LLM calls.
  3. The question mix covers the brief's three edge types plus what real users do: paraphrases and typos, follow-ups, wrong premises (“since we get 20 days of leave…”), near-miss traps like Q15, and questions a given user is not allowed to see answered.
  4. Each run is repeated three times and reported per question, not only as a percentage: with 15 questions one flip moves the score by about 7 points. Keep three or four questions back that you never tune prompts on. Feed the checker a few deliberately wrong answers to measure how often it catches them.
  5. Every failure gets one cause: the earliest stage that went wrong, because an early miss causes the later ones; if no stage went wrong, the cause is a knowledge gap or a test-set error. That is the “why” the brief asks for:
CauseWhat it looks likeWhere the fix goes
Routing missSent to the wrong familyRouter prompt, multi-label routing
Retrieval missThe expected section is not in the top-k Embedding model, k, hybrid (keyword + vector) search
Chunking splitThe answer is cut across two chunksChunk size, heading-aware chunks
Over-filteringThe grader dropped the right chunkGrader prompt, a re-ranker
Generation errorThe right chunk was there; the answer misread or ignored it Generator prompt, model choice
Unsafe answerAnswered a question it should have refused, or a claim not in the chunks got past the checker (the Q15 trap)Generator refusal rule, checker prompt, fail-closed (decision 11)
False refusal or false alarmAn answerable question was refused, or a good answer was regeneratedChecker and refusal rules
Knowledge gapA real question the documents don't answer (seen in pilot logs; in the golden set its expected behaviour is “refuse”)The documents, not the bot: send it to the owner
Test-set errorThe expected answer or source is itself wrong The golden set; confirm with the document owner

A cheap experiment answers part of the “why” today. Re-running 2A's retrieval offline, with the same MiniLM model and the same 55 chunks and no LLM, put the key facts the script checks (one or two per question, such as “15 days of paid annual leave”) in the retrieved chunks for all 12 questions that have a fact to find, both with each question's expected route and with a flat top-4 search. On this corpus, a missing key fact in the full run therefore points to routing, grading or generation, not to vector search. It took one script, and it is the kind of evidence the description's last sentence asks for.

2.10What to take away

  1. The description's last sentence is the deliverable: design the evaluation before the bot, and end with a findings readout.
  2. 2A's biggest additions, the grader, the checker and the fixed refusal, all guard against wrong answers: the checking an FDE builds when a wrong answer is expensive. Then check what happens when those checks can't run.
  3. Expected sources, and a record of every stage, turn “it failed” into “it failed because…”.
  4. Most of 2A's risk sits in questions nobody asked: permissions, changing documents, data residency, fail-open checks.
  5. Test design choices with small experiments: one offline script showed the namespaces add no recall for the facts it checked, at this size; precision is the next thing to measure.
